Autoresearch as Optimization

One self-evolution loop, different ways to use feedback.

Chonghe Jiang5 min read
On this page Search over ideas Memory and restarts The cost of feedback Learning the next experiment

Most autoresearch systems share a self-evolution loop: select a starting point, generate a candidate, evaluate it, and use the result to shape the next attempt. The methods below change particular operations within that loop. The comparison is clearest when we keep the loop fixed and show what each method changes.

Search over ideas

A comment by tsuname connects autoresearch with momentum, cross-entropy search, and simulated annealing. The connection starts with a simple objective: find the best verified candidate within a budget.

\[C_t=\mathcal M(H_t),\qquad x_{t+1}\sim p_\theta(\cdot\mid C_t),\qquad y_{t+1}=f(x_{t+1}).\]

The system selects context $C_t$ from history $H_t$, generates candidate $x$, and evaluates it with $f$. Changing context changes the search distribution, even with fixed model weights.

DeltaEvolve: carry useful changes forward. It records semantic deltas describing how a program changed and whether the change helped. These deltas guide later edits. The momentum analogy concerns accumulated directions of improvement; the system does not compute numerical gradients through programs.

Contrastive Concept-Tree Search: learn where to sample. It represents programs through a concept hierarchy, fits concept distributions to high- and low-performing programs, and guides parent selection with their likelihood ratio. A concept gains weight when it distinguishes strong programs from weak ones. The cross-entropy update changes concept-space sampling statistics, not LLM weights.

Memory and restarts

At the memory step, three methods make different choices about what survives:

Fork-and-Flush keeps the workspace, forks fresh-context continuations, and continues the best branch. Its ablations show that branching and context resets each help, with a larger gain from combining them.

Scaling Discovery Requires Forgetting clears more: agent sessions and the other candidate history. The team restarts from its best candidate and score. Experiments cover heuristic programming, kernel optimization, and model training.

Stateless Language Agents retains research state in the harness and rebuilds every invocation’s context. An Advisor sees evidence across directions and assigns experiments; Workers receive focused local context.

The distinction is between stored evidence and visible context. SLA keeps the record but controls who sees it; Forgetting removes most of the active search history. Neither requires carrying an entire conversation forward.

What survives must also preserve the meaning of a result. SLA distinguishes a measured non-improvement from an execution failure: code that never ran is not evidence that the underlying idea failed.

The cost of feedback

DuoDiscover jointly discovers an optimization target and a cheap proxy evaluator, or yardstick. Workers iterate quickly against the proxy; full evaluations confirm gains and check whether its rankings remain reliable. A drifting yardstick is invalidated and rebuilt, or the worker changes target.

This increases the number of experiments the budget can support. It helps when a faithful proxy exists and the saved evaluation time outweighs its construction cost.

Learning the next experiment

MIRA-AC learns research decisions from their downstream returns. An outer policy selects investigations; a fixed executor carries them out. A shared generative critic estimates remaining return, allowing later gains to provide credit to earlier decisions. In a physics-discovery example, the policy requests a controlled parameter change that lets the executor distinguish two competing interaction laws.

IdeaScientist trains the proposal process instead: gap finding, innovation, and report writing each receive GRPO training with reference-grounded rewards, under a fixed orchestrator. Its output is a research proposal; MIRA-AC learns from executed investigations.

For test-time training, this leaves a concrete choice of target: reward the next candidate’s score, or credit decisions for the progress they enable over several experiments. The second requires preserving the links between decisions and later outcomes, even when agent contexts are reset.
